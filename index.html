Course Preparation With an AI Knowledge Base

We compared how ADS401 postgraduate students used XJTLU AI to prepare for class. The study examined access to verifiable sources, usability and workload.

Research Questions

Main question. How does using XJTLU AI with an ADS401 course knowledge base (KB+) compare with using general XJTLU AI chat (KB−) when postgraduate students prepare for class?

  1. Task performance. Do the two groups differ in the quality of their course content reviews and course designs?
  2. User experience. How do the two groups differ in perceived usability, the ability to find verifiable sources, and willingness to continue using the tool?
  3. Workload and efficiency. How do the two groups differ in perceived workload, time spent on each task, and the number of follow-up questions?
  4. Task suitability. Which tasks benefit most from the course knowledge base, and when do answers lack relevance, accuracy, or sufficient detail?

The current analysis uses 24 post-task questionnaires. It provides evidence about participants’ reported experiences and workload. The blinded-scoring section reports validated rubric ratings of task outputs, scored blind to condition. Chat logs are needed to assess actual task efficiency and where difficulties arose.

Study Design

Both groups used XJTLU AI. The study design specified the same underlying model, task briefs, materials and initial prompts.

KB+ · Course knowledge base

Participants used XJTLU AI with the ADS401 course knowledge base attached.

KB− · General AI chat

Participants used general XJTLU AI chat without a course knowledge base.

Task 1 · Course content review

  • Identify key points and explain unfamiliar concepts.
  • Evaluate an argument and write self-test questions.

Task 2 · Systematic Course Design

Participants received an ADS401-related course or system design brief specifying the target audience, course duration, and topic scope. They used AI to draft a course design that included:

  1. Course objectives and intended learning outcomes.
  2. Content modules and how the knowledge was organised.
  3. Teaching activities and assessment methods.
  4. Supporting materials or rationale for each module.

Pre-task questionnaire. The study design included questions about students’ backgrounds, preparation habits and expectations for AI-assisted preparation.

Post-task questionnaire. Participants rated usability, user experience and workload. The analysis used 24 completed questionnaires and grouped respondents by the chat condition they reported using.

Results

Students in the knowledge-base group reported easier access to verifiable sources. They also rated the system as less usable and reported more workload.

Group means, 12 participants per group. Each bar starts at the minimum of its own scale.

Key Findings

  • Usability and workload. KB+ scored 47.71 points lower on usability and 5.55 points higher on perceived workload. It also reported less intention to continue using the tool.
  • Verifiable sources. KB+ rated its ability to find verifiable sources higher. The item-level differences in perceived course relevance, consistency of terminology and factual accuracy did not reach statistical significance.
  • Task performance. The difference in self-rated performance did not reach statistical significance. For each task, 11 of 12 KB+ participants and 12 of 12 KB− participants reported completion. See Blinded Scoring for the rubric assessment of their work.

Blinded Scoring

We used rubric scores for the submitted work to examine whether the patterns in the questionnaires also appeared in the task outputs.

Scoring status. The ratings below have been reconciled against both raters’ original sheets. All 48 anonymised outputs match the key, every score is a numeric 1–5 value, and all eight disagreements above one point were arbitrated and recorded. The figures are reported as findings rather than provisional results.

Scoring procedure

The scoring workbook records 48 anonymised outputs from 24 participants. Two raters scored the outputs independently with group labels withheld. The five rubric dimensions used a 1–5 scale. Each participant’s two task scores were averaged for the group comparison, with 12 participants per group.

Each output was scored on five dimensions, giving 240 rater-pairs of scores. The two raters worked independently and were compared only afterwards: 232 of the 240 pairs (96.7%) differed by no more than one point, and the eight pairs that differed by more than one point were resolved through a recorded arbitration decision.

Blinding check and rater agreement

Rater A correctly identified 9 of the 18 outputs they guessed were from KB+ (50%, exact binomial p = .593). Rater B correctly identified 11 of 20 (55%, p = .412). KB+ made up 24 of the 48 outputs. Neither rate differed from chance, so the condition was not reliably inferable from the outputs. The two raters also did not differ in severity (mean total 15.33 vs 15.12, Wilcoxon p = .258).

Inter-rater agreement was estimated as a two-way random, absolute-agreement, average-measures ICC — ICC(2,k) — with the 48 outputs as the unit of analysis and 95% confidence intervals from the McGraw & Wong (1996) F method. The total score reached ICC(2,k) = .923 [.889, .941]; dimension-level values ranged from .696 to .831, with three dimensions rating good and two moderate (Koo & Li, 2016). How this relates to the questionnaire. The verifiability scores point in the same direction as students’ self-reported ability to find verifiable sources (r = .50 with item R4), and submission readiness varies with perceived usability (r = .61 with SUS) and ease of use (r = .52). The conditions did not differ in factual accuracy (3.08 vs 3.12, p = 1.000, d = −0.06), so the knowledge base did not make answers more accurate; what it changed was whether a claim could be traced to a specific chapter or page. The trade-off shows up in submission readiness: KB+ outputs were better sourced but needed more work before they could be handed in.

Limitations and Next Steps

Study Limitations

  • Statistical power. With 12 participants per group the study could reliably detect only large effects (α = .05, power = .80, minimum detectable d ≈ 1.20). Structural completeness (d = 0.42) and the other non-significant dimensions should be read as “not detected”, not as evidence of equivalence; no equivalence test was run.
  • Task-level attribution. The scoring key records participant and condition but not which task each output came from, so rubric results are reported at participant level only. A task-by-condition breakdown needs the task labels recovered from the original export order.
  • Study scope. The study covered two ADS401 tasks and 24 completed questionnaires. Most outcomes came from self-reports and describe participants’ experiences in this study.
  • Session effects. Each of the four sessions included six participants who used the same condition. Differences between sessions or their participants may have contributed to the results.

Next Steps

  • Task-level analysis. Recovering the task labels for the 48 outputs would let verifiability and submission readiness be reported separately for the course review and the course design task, which is where the knowledge base is expected to help most.
  • Workload during use. Use chat duration, the number of turns and error logs to identify which steps add workload.
